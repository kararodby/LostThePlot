Capital, Creativity and Control in the Struggle for a Livable World
Jesse Goldstein
From climate denial to delay to deferral… and now increasingly silence, the prevailing means of justifying climate inaction continue to evolve, while underlying environmental conditions deteriorate. As an environmental sociologist I have been researching and writing about various chapters in the history of attempts to 'green' capitalism, from cleantech's non-disruptive disruptions to net zero's climate deferrals, focusing on the stories that we tell ourselves, and that we are told, about how to 'make an impact' or 'save the planet'.

A battle between capital and creativity pits the general intelligence, capabilities and world-making aspirations of regular people against people with institutionalized wealth and power who would prefer to preserve their privileges as opposed to the planet.

We are at an impasse. As emissions continue to rise, we are now facing a powerful veil of climate silence intent on wishing the crisis away in favor of a fresh wave of fossil fueled industrial development. Artificial intelligence and the data centers it requires are being sold as a general purpose technology while their felt effects center on increased costs of living, displacing workers, degrading cognitive capacities and cultural products, and imposing an impenetrable web of surveillance across the world - all with anticipated ecological costs at a scale that's hard to fathom.

In this session I will introduce conceptual tools that help make sense of this long and largely futile attempt to make capitalism greener, while ending with a tentatively optimistic provocation that data center and AI resistance could provide an opportunity to develop a more critical popular assessment of technology as not universally good or always leading towards "progress," which could create an opening for broader engagement with degrowth environmentalism.

Why 'High-Road' Principles Are Critical to Guide the Energy Transition
Priyanka Mohanty and Leslie Chang
September 2026
As the energy transition accelerates, industrial policy will be critical to building political consensus around climate action — but only if it brings labor into the conversation. Drawing on the lessons of the IRA and 20th-century industrial transformation, Priyanka Mohanty and Leslie Chang argue that 'high-road' principles — centering worker participation and community benefit — are essential to building a climate economy that is both durable and just.

Money Makes The World Go Hot: How Extreme Wealth and Inequality Are a Climate Problem
Danielle Levy
August 2026
Extreme wealth isn't just unfair — it's a climate problem. From carbon-intensive lifestyles to investments in carbon-intensive industry, the hoarding and pursuit of wealth is driving the climate crisis. Such wealth is fueling anti-climate politicians and the perpetuation of the petrostate while depriving those most impacted from the funds needed for mitigation and adaptation.

Replicas for Sovereignty: What Tuvalu's Turn to Digital Statehood Teaches Us About Climate Injustice
Bibatshu Thapa Chhetri
July 2026
Faced with rising seas and global inaction, Tuvalu is becoming the world's first digital nation — a brilliant, defiant, and deeply troubling act of fighting for sovereignty against a system that has failed to protect it. This talk explores what's gained and lost when vulnerable nations are forced to reimagine statehood itself, and why the Global South's fight for physical survival deserves a far louder place in climate conversations.
